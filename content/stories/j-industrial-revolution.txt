T: The chain starts long before people. Coal forms when plants decay into peat, and the peat is buried and changed by heat and pressure over millions of years.
T: Vast deposits come from the swampy coal forests that covered much of the tropics in the late Carboniferous. The period's very name means coal-bearing. The camera is over the rocks that are now Britain, where the plate model places them at the time.
K: Coal is made of very, very old plants. Long before the dinosaurs, huge swampy forests covered the warm parts of the world. When the plants died they were buried and squeezed for millions of years, until they turned into black rock.
K: The camera is looking at the land that is now Britain. It was in a different place then.

title: Iron without forests
y: 1709
cam: Coalbrookdale | 1500
src: Abraham Darby I
src: Coalbrookdale
T: Iron was smelted with charcoal, made from wood, and charcoal was getting scarce. At Coalbrookdale in 1709 Abraham Darby got a blast furnace working on coke instead, which is made from coal. It was a major step forward in the production of iron.
K: To make iron, you need a very hot fire. People used charcoal, which is made from wood, and wood was running short. In 1709 Abraham Darby found a way to use coal instead. Now iron could be made in much bigger amounts.

@scene ir-04
story: industrial-revolution
title: A pump for flooded mines
y: 1712
cam: 53.5, -2, 2400
src: Newcomen atmospheric engine
img: File:Newcomen6325.png | A diagram of Newcomen's engine from a 1913 physics textbook. Steam from the boiler fills the cylinder, is condensed, and the air's pressure pushes the piston down.
T: Digging deeper for coal meant digging into water. In 1712 Thomas Newcomen invented an engine that burned coal to make steam, and used it to work a pump. It was the first practical device to harness steam to do mechanical work.
T: Newcomen engines were used mainly to pump water out of mines. Hundreds were built during the 18th century.
K: Deep mines fill with water. In 1712 Thomas Newcomen invented an engine that burned coal to make steam, and the steam worked a pump. It was the first useful steam engine. Hundreds were built to pump water out of mines.

T: Cloth was the first industry to change. James Hargreaves invented the spinning jenny in 1764 to 1765: one worker could now work eight or more spools at once. In 1771 Richard Arkwright built the world's first water-powered cotton spinning mill at Cromford.
T: The first factories ran on water wheels. Steam came to them later.
K: Making cloth changed first. A machine called the spinning jenny let one worker spin eight threads at once instead of one. In 1771 Richard Arkwright built a big mill at Cromford where a water wheel turned the spinning machines.

@scene ir-07
story: industrial-revolution
title: Half the coal
y: 1765
to: 1776
cam: Falkirk | 2600
src: Watt steam engine
T: Newcomen's engine worked, but it ate fuel. In 1765 James Watt had the idea of condensing the steam in a separate chamber. His engine went on sale in 1776, and the first went to the Carron ironworks near Falkirk. It used half as much coal to produce the same power.
T: Later versions could turn a wheel, and Watt's partner Matthew Boulton developed machines to use that rotary power. Engines could now drive machinery, not only pumps.
K: The first steam engines burned a lot of coal. James Watt made a better one. It needed only half the coal to do the same work. Later it could turn wheels, so it could run the machines in a factory.

K: Now there was plenty of iron. In 1781 a bridge made of iron opened over the River Severn, the first big iron bridge in the world.

T: Not everyone gained. The Luddites were English textile workers who protested against certain kinds of automated machinery, out of concern for pay, child labour, working conditions and the quality of the work. They often destroyed machines in organised raids.
T: The movement began at Arnold in Nottinghamshire on 11 March 1811 and spread to Yorkshire and Lancashire.
K: The new machines did not make everyone happy. Some cloth workers were afraid for their pay and their jobs. They were called Luddites, and they smashed machines at night. It began near Nottingham in 1811.

T: Burning coal has a cost. From 5 to 9 December 1952, cold windless weather trapped smoke, mostly from coal, over London. Government reports at the time put the deaths at up to 4,000. More recent research suggests 10,000 to 12,000.
T: The disaster led to the Clean Air Act of 1956.
K: Burning coal makes dirty air. In December 1952 thick smoke hung over London for five days. Thousands of people died from breathing it. Afterwards, a new law was made to clean up the air.

K: Burning coal also makes a gas you cannot see, called carbon dioxide. In 1958 a scientist named Charles David Keeling began measuring it on a mountain in Hawaii. His numbers showed that there was more of it in the air every year.

@scene ir-15
story: industrial-revolution
title: Still burning
y: 2024
cam: 25, 80, 30000
src: Coal
T: The chain has not ended. In 2020 coal supplied about a quarter of the world's primary energy and over a third of its electricity. A record amount was burnt in 2024, releasing over fifteen billion tonnes of carbon dioxide.
T: Coal is the largest single source of the carbon dioxide that is changing the climate. The forests of the Carboniferous took millions of years to bury that carbon. We are returning it to the air in a few centuries.
K: People still burn coal today, more than ever. About a third of the world's electricity comes from it. Burning coal is the biggest cause of the gas that is warming the Earth.
K: It took millions of years for the old forests to turn into coal. We are burning it up in a few hundred years.
