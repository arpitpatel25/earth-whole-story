T: On this globe the plate model puts India far out in the ocean, on its way north. On it, lava was pouring out in floods. T: Some scientists have argued that the gases from these eruptions played a major part in the extinction. Most now think the eruptions played a minor role at most.

K: Some scientists think the gas from these volcanoes helped kill the dinosaurs. Most think it mattered only a little.

T: At a site called Tanis, in today's North Dakota, a research team reports a record of the first hour. By their account the earthquake waves arrived within about ten minutes and set the water surging. Fish were buried with tiny droplets of melted rock from the impact caught in their gills.
T: Growth patterns in the fish bones suggest that it was spring. These findings rest on the Tanis team's own publications and are still being tested by others.
K: Far to the north, in today's North Dakota, scientists found fish that seem to have died that very day. Tiny beads of melted rock from the crash were stuck in their gills.
K: The fish bones even hint at the season. It was probably spring. Other scientists are still checking these discoveries.

@scene dino-08
story: dinosaur-extinction
title: The long dark
y: 66 mya
cam: 10, -60, 30000
src: Chicxulub crater
src: Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event
T: The asteroid hit rock rich in sulfur and vaporised it into the sky. Dust and soot blocked the sunlight for a decade or longer, and the surface cooled sharply.
T: Without light, photosynthesis stopped in plants and in the plankton of the sea. Food chains collapsed from the bottom up.
K: The crash threw so much dust and soot into the sky that sunlight could not get through for ten years or more. It got very cold.
K: Plants need sunlight to live. When the plants died, the plant eaters starved. Then the meat eaters starved too.

title: A clue in the clay
y: 1980
cam: Gubbio | 2600
src: Alvarez hypothesis
src: Cretaceous–Paleogene boundary
img: File:LWA with Walt.JPG | Luis Alvarez (left) and his son Walter Alvarez at the boundary layer in the Bottaccione Gorge near Gubbio, Italy.
img: File:Cretaceous Paleogene clay at Geulhemmergroeve.jpg | The clay layer between Cretaceous and Paleogene rocks, in the Geulhemmergroeve tunnels near Geulhem in the Netherlands.
T: For a long time nobody knew why the dinosaurs vanished. Then in 1980 a team led by the physicist Luis Alvarez, with his son the geologist Walter Alvarez and the chemists Frank Asaro and Helen Vaughn Michel, found something strange in the rocks.
T: All over the world, the thin layer that marks the end of the Cretaceous holds hundreds of times more iridium than normal. Iridium is rare in Earth's crust and more common in asteroids. They proposed that a large asteroid had hit the Earth.
K: For a long time nobody knew why the dinosaurs disappeared. In 1980 a father and son, Luis and Walter Alvarez, and two chemists found a clue in a thin layer of rock.
K: The layer was full of a rare metal called iridium. There is not much iridium in Earth's rocks, but there is more in rocks from space. So they said: a giant space rock must have hit the Earth.

y: 1978
to: 2016
cam: Chicxulub crater | 2400
linear: 1
src: Chicxulub crater
img: File:Yucatan chix crater.jpg | A shaded relief image of the Yucatán Peninsula from NASA radar data. The faint arc is the rim of the buried Chicxulub crater.
T: The crater had already been spotted. In 1978 the geophysicists Glen Penfield and Antonio Camargo, surveying for the Mexican oil company Pemex, found signs of a huge buried structure on the edge of the Yucatán. Penfield could not prove it was a crater at the time. In 1990 he and the researcher Hildebrand secured old drill samples, and tests on them clearly showed rock shocked by an impact.
T: The crater is about 200 kilometres across and lies under about a kilometre of younger rock. In 2016 a drilling expedition brought up the first cores from its inner ring of peaks.
K: The hole was hiding under Mexico. Two scientists looking for oil found signs of it in 1978. It took until 1990 to show that it was the crater.
K: The crater is about 200 kilometres wide, but you cannot see it. It is buried under a kilometre of newer rock.
