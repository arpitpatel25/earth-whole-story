@scene mm-02

title: The oldest mine: red paint
y: 43 kya
to: 41 kya
cam: Ngwenya Mine | 3800
src: Ngwenya Mine
T: The Ngwenya Mine in Eswatini is considered the world's oldest. Radiocarbon dating puts its earliest mining at 41,000 to 43,000 years ago.
T: The miners were not after metal. They dug haematite to make red ochre, a pigment. Ochre from here was used by the ancestors of the San in rock paintings. Much later, the same deposit was mined for iron.
K: The oldest mine we know is in Eswatini, in southern Africa. People dug there more than 40,000 years ago. They were not looking for metal. They wanted a red rock to grind into paint.

@scene mm-03

title: Shafts for flint
y: 2600 BCE
to: 2300 BCE
cam: Grime's Graves | 2200
src: Grime's Graves
T: Stone itself was worth digging for. At Grime's Graves in Norfolk, Neolithic miners sank at least 433 shafts through the chalk to reach seams of flint, which was much in demand for polished stone axes. The site was worked between about 2600 and 2300 BC.
T: The largest shafts are more than 14 metres deep. Opening one meant moving more than 2,000 tonnes of chalk, work calculated at five months for twenty men.
K: Good stone was worth digging for too. In England, people dug more than 400 deep holes through the chalk to reach flint, a stone that makes sharp axes. The biggest holes are more than 14 metres deep.

@scene mm-06

title: Tin, and the first long supply chain
y: 1320 BCE
cam: Uluburun shipwreck | 5200
src: Tin sources and trade during antiquity
src: Uluburun shipwreck
T: Copper mixed with tin makes bronze. But tin is rare: about two parts per million of the Earth's crust, against 70 for copper. Ancient sources were far apart: Afghanistan, Cornwall and Devon, Brittany, Spain. Getting hold of it was an important concern from the Bronze Age onward.
T: A ship that sank off Uluburun, on the coast of today's Turkey, in the late 14th century BC shows that trade in mid-voyage. Its main cargoes were ingots of copper and tin, with resin, ceramics and raw glass: more than 20 tons in all.
K: Mix copper with tin and you get bronze. But tin is rare, and the places where it was found were far apart.
K: One of those ships sank near Turkey about 3,300 years ago. Divers found it full of copper and tin.

@scene mm-07
story: mining-and-metals
title: Iron, the common metal
y: 1100 BCE
cam: 30, 50, 12000
src: Ferrous metallurgy
src: Tin sources and trade during antiquity
T: The earliest iron objects, from 4th-millennium BC Egypt, were hammered from meteorites. Nobody knows when or where people first smelted iron from ore. But by the end of the 2nd millennium BC iron was being made from ores across the region from Greece to India.
T: Iron ore is everywhere: iron makes up about 50,000 parts per million of the crust. The raw material was no longer rare.
K: The first iron that people used fell from the sky, in meteorites. Later they learned to get iron out of rock. Iron is far more common in the Earth's rocks than copper or tin.

@scene mm-08
story: mining-and-metals
title: Steel from southern India
y: 400 BCE
cam: 12, 78, 4200
src: Wootz steel
src: Ferrous metallurgy
T: Steel is iron with some carbon in it: more than wrought iron, less than pig iron. Wootz steel, made in crucibles, was developed in southern India in the middle of the 1st millennium BC. It was made in today's Telangana, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu and Sri Lanka.
T: By the 4th century BC southern India was exporting it to China, Africa, the Middle East and Europe.
K: Iron with a little carbon in it is called steel. Smiths in southern India worked out how to make very good steel in small clay pots about 2,500 years ago. It was sold as far away as China and Europe.

cam: Las Médulas | 1600
src: Las Médulas
img: File:Panorámica de Las Médulas.jpg | The landscape of Las Médulas in north-west Spain, left by Roman gold mining.
T: Las Médulas in Spain was the most important gold mine in the Roman Empire, and its largest open pit. The method, described by Pliny the Elder in 77 AD, was called ruina montium, the wrecking of mountains: water brought by at least seven long aqueducts was used to undermine the hillsides.
T: The spectacular landscape it left is still there.
K: The Romans dug for gold in Spain in a huge way. They brought water along long channels and used it to wash whole hillsides away. The strange hills they left behind are still there.

y: 1856
cam: 53.5, -1.5, 3000
src: Bessemer process
T: Steel stayed expensive until 1856, when the Englishman Henry Bessemer patented a new process. Air blown through molten pig iron burned out the excess carbon. It was the first inexpensive industrial process for mass-producing steel.
K: For a long time steel was costly and made in small amounts. In 1856 Henry Bessemer found a way to make a lot of it cheaply, by blowing air through melted iron.

@scene mm-14
story: mining-and-metals
title: A metal made with electricity
y: 1886
cam: 45, -30, 20000
src: Aluminium
src: Hall–Héroult process
T: Aluminium is the third most abundant element in the Earth's crust, but it is virtually never found as a free metal. In 1886 the French engineer Paul Héroult and the American engineer Charles Martin Hall independently developed a way to win it from its oxide with electric current.
T: The process still uses a great deal of electrical energy. It is the reason aluminium became an everyday metal.
K: Aluminium is one of the most common things in the Earth's rocks, but it is very hard to get out. In 1886 two engineers, one French and one American, each found the same way to do it, using electricity. Now aluminium is used everywhere in everyday life.
