@scene zero-03
story: zero
title: Babylon leaves a gap
y: 1500 BCE
to: 300 BCE
cam: Babylon | 3800
src: 0
src: Babylonian cuneiform numerals
T: By the middle of the 2nd millennium BC, Babylonian scribes had a sophisticated number system in base 60, written with a stylus on clay. It was positional: where a sign stood decided what it was worth.
T: An empty position was shown only by a space. By 300 BC a punctuation sign, two slanted wedges, had been repurposed to mark the empty place. It was a placeholder, not yet a number.
K: In Babylon, people wrote numbers by pressing marks into soft clay. Where a mark stood changed what it was worth, just like the 2 in 20 and the 2 in 200.
K: When a place was empty they left a gap. Later they used two slanted marks to say "nothing here".

src: Maya numerals

T: On the other side of the world, people reached the same idea independently. The Long Count calendar of Mesoamerica needed a zero as a placeholder in its base-20 system. The earliest Long Count date known, on Stela 2 at Chiapa de Corzo, is from 36 BC.
T: The Maya wrote zero as a shell. Who used it first is uncertain: the earliest dates lie outside the Maya homeland.
K: Far away in Central America, people invented zero all by themselves. They needed it for their calendar. The Maya drew zero as a shell, one as a dot and five as a bar.

@scene zero-06
story: zero
title: Ten symbols in India
y: 100
to: 400
cam: 23, 79, 6500
src: Hindu–Arabic numeral system
src: Bakhshali manuscript
T: Between the 1st and 4th centuries, Indian mathematicians invented the system we use now: base ten, positional, with one symbol for each digit. The shapes of today's digits descend from Brahmi numerals.
T: The Bakhshali manuscript, a mathematical text on birch bark found in 1881, uses a dot to hold an empty place. The dot was called shunya-bindu, the dot of the empty place. The manuscript's age is disputed: in 2024 Oxford University revised its dating to 799–1102 CE.
K: In India, mathematicians invented a new way to write numbers. Ten symbols were enough for any number, however big, because the place of each symbol tells you what it is worth.
K: For an empty place they first used a dot.

T: Brahmagupta was born in 598 in Bhillamala, today's Bhinmal in Rajasthan, and later moved to Ujjain, a major centre for astronomy. Around 628 he wrote the Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta.
T: It is the first book that gives rules for doing arithmetic with zero and with negative numbers. A positive number added to an equal negative number, it says, makes zero. On one point he differs from us: he defined zero divided by zero as zero, while modern mathematics leaves division by zero undefined.
K: A mathematician named Brahmagupta, born in Rajasthan in the year 598, did something new. He treated zero as a real number, one you can add and subtract like any other, and he wrote down the rules.
K: He also wrote rules for numbers less than zero.

T: By the 9th century Arabic mathematicians had adopted the Indian system and extended it to fractions. Al-Khwarizmi, who worked at the House of Wisdom in Baghdad around 820, described it in On the Calculation with Hindu Numerals about 825. Al-Kindi wrote on the same subject about 830.
T: The Sanskrit word for the empty place, śūnya, was translated by the Arabic word ṣifr, which had meant "empty".
K: Scholars in Baghdad learned the Indian numbers and wrote books explaining them. The most famous was by al-Khwarizmi, around the year 825.
K: The Indian word for zero meant "empty". The Arabic word they chose for it, sifr, meant "empty" too.

T: In the 12th century Latin translations of al-Khwarizmi's textbook, titled Algorithmo de Numero Indorum, brought the decimal positional system to the Western world.
T: The word travelled with the idea. Arabic ṣifr became Italian zefiro, shortened in Venetian to zevero and then zero, and passed through French into English.
K: Next the books were turned into Latin, the language of scholars in Europe. The word travelled too. Arabic "sifr" became Italian "zefiro", which was squeezed down to "zero".

K: A boy from Pisa in Italy grew up in Bugia, a port in what is now Algeria, where his father ran a trading post. There he learned the Indian numbers. Later he was called Fibonacci.
