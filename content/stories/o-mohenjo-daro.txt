T: Mohenjo-daro was built around 2500 BCE beside the Indus, in today's Sindh in Pakistan. With at least 40,000 people it was one of the world's earliest major cities, as old as the cities of Egypt and Mesopotamia.
T: Its people left signs on seals and pots, but nobody has deciphered them. So this is a day reconstructed from walls, wells and things lost in the dust. Where we do not know, the story says so.
K: 4,500 years ago, a great city stood beside the Indus River, in what is now Pakistan. About 40,000 people lived there.
K: We cannot read their writing, so nobody knows their names or their stories. But we can see their houses, their wells and their toys. Let us walk through one day.

K: Many houses had a place to wash and even a toilet. You flushed it by pouring in a jar of water.

@scene md-04
story: mohenjo-daro
title: Streets on a grid, drains under cover
y: 2500 BCE
cam: Mohenjo-daro | 1000
src: Mohenjo-daro
src: Sanitation of the Indus Valley Civilisation
T: Step outside and the city is planned: straight-sided buildings laid out on a grid. Waste water from the houses ran into covered drains along the major streets. The drains had openings at regular intervals for cleaning and inspection.
T: These are among the first known examples of city sanitation anywhere.
K: The streets were straight and crossed each other like the lines on squared paper. Dirty water ran away through covered drains beside the street. These are some of the earliest city drains known anywhere.

T: The city had a central marketplace. Traders used standardised weights, and small stamp seals, most only two or three centimetres across, cut with signs and pressed into clay.
T: More than 400 different signs are known from about 4,000 inscribed objects. Most inscriptions are extremely short, and whether the signs record a language at all is still argued over.
K: In the middle of the city was a market. Traders used weights that were the same everywhere. They also used little seals, only two or three centimetres across, to press a mark into clay.
K: The seals carry signs that look like writing. Nobody today can read them.

T: Craftspeople depended on things from far away. Trade networks reached distant sources of raw materials, among them lapis lazuli and other stones for bead-making. Copper came from Rajasthan and from as far as Oman.
T: Goods probably moved in bullock carts like those still seen across South Asia, and in small flat-bottomed boats on the river.
K: The city's craftspeople made beads from coloured stones. Some of the stones, and the copper for tools, came from very far away. They probably travelled in ox carts and in flat boats on the river.

src: Mohenjo-daro

T: On the high mound called the Citadel stood the Great Bath: a pool about 12 metres by 7 and up to 2.4 metres deep. Its floor was made watertight with finely fitted bricks set in gypsum plaster, and its sides were sealed with a thick layer of bitumen.
T: Most scholars agree it was used for special religious functions in which water purified the bathers. No text says so.
K: On a high mound stood a big brick pool, 12 metres long, with steps leading down into it. The builders sealed it with tar so it would not leak.
K: Most experts think people bathed here for religious reasons. But we cannot be sure.

T: Who planned the grid and the drains? There is no conclusive evidence of palaces or temples. Some houses were larger than others, but Indus cities are remarkable for their apparent, if relative, egalitarianism.
T: Who governed, and how, is unknown.
K: Somebody must have planned the streets and the drains. But no palace for a king has been found, and no great temple. Some houses were larger than others, but the city seems to have been unusually equal. Who ran it is still a mystery.

@scene md-12
story: mohenjo-daro
title: Found again
y: 1920
cam: Mohenjo-daro | 3000
src: Mohenjo-daro
T: The ruins lay undocumented for around 3,700 years. In 1919–1920 R. D. Banerji, an officer of the Archaeological Survey of India, visited the mound. He came to see what he took for a Buddhist stupa, and found a flint scraper that convinced him the place was far older.
T: The name Mohenjo-daro is Sindhi, and means "Mound of the Dead Men". What its own people called their city is not known.
K: For about 3,700 years nobody knew the city was there. Then, about a hundred years ago, an Indian archaeologist named R. D. Banerji visited the mound and realised it was very, very old.
K: We still do not know what the people who lived there called their city.
