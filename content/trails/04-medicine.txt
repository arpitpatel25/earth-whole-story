T: In August 1716 Lady Mary Wortley Montagu went with her husband, the new British ambassador, to Constantinople. She is known for introducing and advocating smallpox inoculation in Britain after her return from Turkey. The method, variolation, used material taken from a smallpox patient in the hope of causing a mild but protective infection.
K: An English lady, Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, travelled to Constantinople in 1716. When she came home she taught people in Britain a way to protect themselves from a deadly disease called smallpox, by giving them a mild case of it on purpose.

T: Onesimus was an African man enslaved by the Boston clergyman Cotton Mather. In 1716 or shortly before, he described to Mather how he had been inoculated against smallpox in Africa. When an outbreak began in Boston in 1721, Mather used that knowledge to argue for inoculation.
T: The method worked, at a price: 1 to 2 percent of those variolated died, against 30 percent of those who caught smallpox naturally.
K: Onesimus was an African man who was enslaved in Boston. He explained how people in his homeland protected themselves from smallpox. When the disease struck Boston in 1721, his knowledge helped to save lives.

T: Edward Jenner, a physician born in Berkeley in Gloucestershire, created the smallpox vaccine, the world's first vaccine. The words vaccine and vaccination come from Variolae vaccinae, "pustules of the cow", his term for cowpox, which he used in the title of his Inquiry in 1798.
K: An English doctor named Edward Jenner made the world's first vaccine. It protected people from smallpox, and it came from cowpox, a disease of cows. The word "vaccine" comes from the Latin word for cow.

title: Wash your hands
date: 1847
at: Vienna
alt: 1500
src: Ignaz Semmelweis
T: At the Vienna General Hospital, the doctors' maternity wards had three times the death rate of the midwives' wards. In 1847 Ignaz Semmelweis proposed that doctors wash their hands in a chlorinated lime solution. The death rate of mothers fell from 18 percent to less than 2 percent. He is remembered as the "saviour of mothers".
K: In a hospital in Vienna, many mothers were dying after having their babies. A doctor named Ignaz Semmelweis told the other doctors to wash their hands before touching a patient. Far fewer mothers died.

T: In 1854 cholera broke out around Broad Street in Soho, London. The physician John Snow traced it to one public water pump and argued that germ-contaminated water was the cause. He is considered one of the founders of modern epidemiology.
K: In 1854 many people in one part of London fell ill with a disease called cholera. Dr John Snow worked out that they had all drunk water from the same pump. The water was carrying the disease.

His vaccines for rabies and anthrax are credited with saving millions of lives. In Paris in 1887 he established the Pasteur Institute, which he directed for the rest of his life.
K: Louis Pasteur was a French scientist who studied germs. He made vaccines against two deadly diseases, rabies and anthrax. Pasteurisation is named after him.

T: Jonas Salk, a professor at the University of Pittsburgh from 1947, developed one of the first successful polio vaccines. When its success was made public in April 1955 he was hailed as a miracle worker. He chose not to patent the vaccine or seek any profit from it, so that it could be distributed as widely as possible.
K: Polio, once called infantile paralysis, was a feared disease. In 1955 the world learned that Dr Jonas Salk's vaccine worked. He did not try to make money from it, so that it could reach as many people as possible.
